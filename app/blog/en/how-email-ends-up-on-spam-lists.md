## How Your Email Address Ends Up on Spam Lists (and How to Stop It)

Spam rarely starts with a lucky guess. In most cases the address was handed over, passed on, or leaked. Once you know the routes, closing them is much easier.

### The five common routes

1. **Sign-up forms.** Creating an account, downloading a PDF, or joining a Wi-Fi network often includes consent to marketing email, sometimes from "selected partners" as well. The box may already be ticked.
2. **Data breaches.** When a company's user database is stolen, the addresses in it end up in collections that are traded and reused for spam and phishing.
3. **Sharing and selling.** Some businesses share customer lists with partners or sell them. Lists also change hands when a company is bought or shuts down.
4. **Public pages.** An address written on a website, a forum profile, or a public document can be collected by automated tools.
5. **Guessing.** Senders try common names at large mail providers. This is less common than the other four, but it happens.

### Why it gets worse over time

An address is almost never removed from a list. Lists are merged, resold, and leaked again, so a sign-up from years ago can still be producing mail today. And your main address is hard to change, because everything important is attached to it.

That is why prevention matters more than clean-up: the useful question is not "how do I stop this sender" but "how many places have my real address".

### Which address to give

Sort websites into three groups and give each group a different kind of address.

- **A temporary address, for things you will never come back to.** One-time downloads, trials, coupons, an article behind a form. There is nothing to unsubscribe from later, because you never look at that inbox again. On smail.pw it takes one button and [no registration](/temporary-email-no-registration).
- **An email alias, for accounts you keep but do not fully trust.** Shops, apps, newsletters you actually read. An alias forwards to your real inbox. If it starts receiving junk, you switch that one alias off, and you also know who passed it on.
- **Your real address, for the few accounts that matter.** Banks, work, government, healthcare, and any account used to recover the others. Because so few places have it, it stays clean.

[Temporary email vs email alias](/blog/temporary-email-vs-email-alias) compares the first two in more detail.

### Habits that keep an address clean

- **Read the checkboxes.** Untick marketing consent, and look for the word "partners".
- **Unsubscribe from senders you recognise.** Legitimate companies honour the unsubscribe link.
- **Report the rest as spam.** With a sender you have never dealt with, clicking anything in the message, including "unsubscribe", can confirm that your address is in use. Use your mail provider's spam button instead.
- **Do not reply to spam,** for the same reason.
- **Do not publish your address in plain text.** On a public page, a contact form is safer than a written address.
- **Check for breaches.** Free breach notification services such as Have I Been Pwned tell you whether an address appears in known leaks. If it does, change the passwords of the affected accounts.

### If your main address is already flooded

- Use the spam button every time. Filters learn from it.
- Unsubscribe from every sender you recognise in one sitting. It is tedious once and pays off for a long time.
- Create filters for whatever is left.
- Stop the inflow: from now on, give your real address only to the third group above.

If the address is beyond saving, moving your important accounts to a new one and leaving the old one for everything else is slow, but it is the fix that lasts.

### Final takeaway

Spam follows the address. The fewer places that hold your real one, the less of it arrives. Use a temporary address for one-offs, an alias for ongoing accounts, and your real address for the few that matter.
